# How to Hold a Cockroach

## A fable about the stories that become reality

Matthew Maxwell’s illustrated fable begins with a boy repelled by a cockroach and expands that small encounter into a question about an entire life: how much of what seems inherent in the world is meaning we have learned to give it? The answer is not simply that everything should be viewed positively. The deeper proposal is that recognising the constructed character of a story creates freedom in how experience is held.

Adler distinguishes imaginative literature from expository argument. This book should therefore be read first as a designed experience: recurring scenes allow the reader to feel a pattern before naming it. Its claims can subsequently be reconstructed and criticised, but reducing it immediately to “change your mindset” misses the work done by the fable.

## The structure is part of the teaching

The first part, A Boy and His Stories, contains twelve encounters. The boy confronts the cockroach, himself, love, the past, the future, doing, others, death, life, emotion, knowing and everything. The repeated structure is deliberate: something appears intolerable, a remembered experience helps explain its meaning, and the boy begins to see that his interpretation is not the thing itself.

The second part, A New World, moves through Nothing, Being and The Choice. This is a change in mode. The story does not end with a better theory of each problem. It passes through the loosening of familiar meanings into immediate perception, then returns to interpretation as an activity in which compassion can participate. The final choice therefore matters as much as the initial unlearning.

## What the cockroach stands for

The cockroach is initially experienced as if disgust were its fixed property. Recalling his mother’s reaction lets the boy see how this apparent fact was learned. The same device is then applied to much more consequential subjects. The past and future appear as emotionally charged constructions, while the self becomes a story assembled from remembered encounters.

“To hold” is the book’s crucial term. It concerns the relationship to an experience, not necessarily physically holding an insect. “Story” means the interpretation that makes events signify rejection, danger, failure or possibility. “Freedom” is the possibility of recognising that interpretation and responding differently. It does not mean controlling which events occur.

## The argument carried by the narrative

The implied reasoning is that experience contains both an event and a learned interpretation; repeated confirmation makes the interpretation seem inevitable; seeing its origin weakens that inevitability; and compassion becomes possible where automatic condemnation once operated. The closing movement toward love is consequently more than positive thinking. The boy recognises the suffering involved in the earlier interpretations and meets it differently.

The recurring language creates a useful transfer effect. Once the reader recognises the pattern around a cockroach, its reappearance around the future or other people is harder to ignore. Literary repetition performs the generalisation that a philosophical essay might express in premises.

The ending also avoids a purely destructive scepticism. If meanings are constructed, it does not follow that nothing matters. Meaning can be held with greater awareness and care. The book moves from captivity inside an interpretation to participation in interpretation.

## What deserves scrutiny

The strongest insight is the distinction between an occurrence and the conclusion drawn from it. That distinction creates room for a less punitive relationship with memory and identity. The book is especially effective when it makes a supposedly obvious judgement look contingent without humiliating the person who learned it.

Its weakness is the simplicity required by the fable. Real histories rarely resolve as cleanly as a single remembered scene followed by recognition. An interpretation can be constructed and still track a real danger. A person may gain freedom in their response without gaining control over their circumstances. The affirmation of choice is best read as an opening, not as a judgement that everyone could instantly choose away distress.

The narrative also does not prove that all meaning is arbitrary. It demonstrates, through its imagined world, that some meanings can change. The universal conclusion would need an argument beyond the illustration. This is an important boundary between the fable’s persuasive power and its evidential power.

## Reading it again

Notice which repeated episode feels obvious and which feels objectionable. That difference may reveal where your own interpretation feels inseparable from reality. Write the event, your meaning and one alternative meaning in separate sentences. Then ask what facts must remain acknowledged regardless of interpretation.

Compare Awareness, which challenges attachment through direct provocation, and You Were Never Broken, which uses poetry to soften rejection of experience. Maxwell’s distinctive achievement is to make a philosophical shift emotionally legible through a simple character and repeated plot. Return to the final choice after the early chapters: freedom is incomplete if it only dismantles stories and never asks how to live with other people.
