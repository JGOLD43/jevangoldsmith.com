# You Were Never Broken

## Poetry against the verdict of defectiveness

Jeff Foster’s collection asks whether painful experience really proves that a person is fundamentally wrong. Its answer is an insistent welcome to the parts of life that self-improvement often tries to eliminate. The poems and prose invite the reader to stop turning vulnerability into a verdict against their existence.

Adler’s rules for imaginative literature are particularly important here. A poem is not a compressed clinical claim. Its voice, rhythm, images and emotional movement contribute to its meaning. The reader should first inhabit the offered perspective, then ask what it illuminates and where literal interpretation would overreach.

## The collection’s shape

The locally available text opens with Everything Is Welcome and moves through four parts: A Field of Meditation, Saying Yes to the Mess, The Courage to Stand Alone, and Reasons to Stay Alive. This arc moves from immediate attention through acceptance and independence toward an affirmation of participation in life.

The form is deliberately spacious. Short lines and brief pieces interrupt the momentum of habitual explanation. The reader is repeatedly brought back to breath, body, uncertainty and the present encounter. The repetition performs a function: permission may need to be encountered more than once before it feels available.

## What brokenness means

“Broken” operates at more than one level. It can describe genuine pain or disruption, but it can also become an identity: I am a defective person because this is happening. Foster’s title contests the second meaning without requiring denial of the first.

“Acceptance” concerns allowing experience to be acknowledged. “Meditation” is presented as attention without an imposed goal of producing a superior state. “Welcome” even includes the part that does not want to welcome anything. This recursive generosity is central: resistance does not become a final exception that disqualifies the reader.

The language of wholeness is therefore best read as a claim about belonging and dignity, not a statement that every bodily or psychological difficulty is imaginary.

## The argument within the invitation

The underlying reasoning is that a painful state can be met with a further judgement of failure; that judgement adds isolation and struggle; demands for spiritual improvement can repeat the same rejection; and a nonjudgemental welcome changes the relationship to the experience even before the experience changes.

The opening prose on meditation makes the point concrete by directing attention toward the ordinary fact of holding and reading a book. The sought-after spiritual event is replaced by immediate sensory life. This is a literary and practical move: the text refuses to let the reader postpone attention until a more impressive moment.

The poems’ direct address also matters. They create the feeling of a compassionate interlocutor. Instead of arguing abstractly that all experience is permissible, they speak as though the reader’s particular unfinished life is already included. The persuasive force is relational as much as conceptual.

## How to judge the poetry fairly

The collection succeeds when it loosens shame without denying pain. Its accessibility gives readers a language for experiences that more abstract nondual philosophy can leave untouched. The best lines invite a change in stance rather than demand agreement with a theory.

The main limitation arises when consoling language is treated as a universal explanation. Saying that a moment belongs does not mean every event is right, every action harmless or every condition in no need of care. “Never broken” should not be used to tell someone their injury or distress is unreal. Dignity and the need for help can coexist.

Nor can a collection of poems establish that its approach will produce a particular psychological outcome. The book can accompany a reader; it cannot guarantee transformation. That distinction protects the work’s literary value from claims it cannot substantiate.

Its affirmation of standing alone also needs a relational counterweight. Independence from approval is not the same as refusing support. A less conditional relationship to oneself can make receiving care easier rather than unnecessary.

## Returning to the collection

Read a single piece aloud, allowing its pace to differ from ordinary scanning. Notice which word or image changes the felt meaning. Then paraphrase its invitation in one plain sentence. If your paraphrase becomes a demand—“I should accept everything immediately”—return to the poem and see what gentleness was lost.

A useful second question is what the poem does not claim. Permission to feel anger does not justify cruelty. Permission to rest does not decide every obligation. This distinction lets the poems retain emotional breadth without becoming indiscriminate instructions.

Compare The Way of Rest, where Foster develops a larger collection of related themes, and Comfortable with Uncertainty, which brings a Buddhist practice framework to a similar friendliness. Their meeting point is acceptance without self-abandonment. The most fruitful rereading leaves you less eager to issue a verdict about yourself and more able to inhabit an ordinary, imperfect day.
