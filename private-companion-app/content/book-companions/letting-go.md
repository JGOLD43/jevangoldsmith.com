# Letting Go: The Pathway of Surrender

## The problem Hawkins wants to solve

David R. Hawkins asks why emotional suffering persists despite attempts to explain, suppress, express or escape it. His proposed answer is surrender: allow a feeling to be experienced without feeding its story or fighting its presence. The central practical idea is that resistance can prolong the struggle with an emotion.

The book combines a method of emotional observation with a spiritual hierarchy of consciousness and far-reaching claims about health and reality. Adler’s analytical method requires separating these components. A useful observation about feelings does not automatically validate a numerical scale of consciousness or a physiological explanation attached to it.

## How the book develops

Hawkins introduces letting go, explores emotions across a hierarchy ranging from constricted states toward more expansive ones, and applies the method to relationships, work, bodily life and spiritual development. This arrangement makes each emotion both a subject of inquiry and a position within an overall ascent.

The book’s emotional chapters encourage attention to the payoffs of holding on. Resentment, for example, may hurt while also preserving the satisfaction of being right. This is an especially important move: the difficulty is not always that we lack a technique. We may be reluctant to relinquish what the suffering appears to give us.

## What surrender means here

“Surrender” is not simply resignation or outward compliance. It refers to releasing resistance to the felt experience itself. “Suppression” pushes the feeling out of awareness; “escape” distracts from it; “expression” can discharge it while leaving the underlying pattern intact. Hawkins treats direct allowing as a distinct possibility.

The “payoff” of an emotion is the benefit we may obtain from retaining it—identity, superiority, protection or an excuse. “Love”, in the examined chapter, is described as a way of being expressed through generosity, care and ordinary acts of service. This broadens the goal beyond merely feeling better.

His calibrated “levels of consciousness” are a separate set of claims. Numbers imply measurement, and measurement requires a reliable procedure. The book’s confidence in its scale should not be mistaken for evidence that the scale is scientifically established.

## Reconstructing the practical argument

An emotion arises; we interpret it as intolerable or identifying; resistance and repetitive thought intensify the encounter; and the intensified experience appears to confirm the need for resistance. Allowing interrupts the loop. Attention shifts from proving the story to noticing the feeling and the impulse to control it.

The book’s example of resentment over an unpaid loan makes the hidden payoff concrete. The creditor can become attached to the moral advantage of being wronged. A reader need not accept every claimed consequence of surrender to recognise the possibility. Giving up the pleasure of superiority could change the quality of a conversation while leaving the debt and the need for boundaries intact.

This distinction is fundamental. Letting go of a feeling is not the same as cancelling a practical obligation. One can pursue repayment without rehearsing resentment. Conversely, a calmer feeling does not establish that a disputed action was acceptable.

## What is strong and what is unsupported

Hawkins is persuasive when he asks readers to observe their investment in an emotional pattern. The method also gives a useful alternative to the assumption that every feeling must immediately become an explanation or an action. His chapter on love connects inner change to concrete generosity, which provides a better test than a claimed spiritual rank.

The account overreaches when muscle testing is treated as a means of verifying truth, or when broad claims about consciousness and health are presented as if the practical method had established them. The inference is missing. Feeling relief does not demonstrate the proposed mechanism, establish the numerical hierarchy or show that illness is caused by insufficient surrender.

The book also risks becoming self-sealing: if surrender works, the theory is praised; if it does not, the reader may be said to be resisting. A fair test must allow for the possibility that a method is limited, a claim mistaken or a situation in need of a different response. The author’s framework should remain accountable to experience rather than explaining away every difficulty.

## A disciplined rereading

Read with two columns: practical observation and explanatory claim. Put the felt experience of releasing tension in the first; put assertions about energetic levels or physical causation in the second. Agreement in one column does not require agreement in the other.

Notice whether “letting go” has become another demand: I should not feel this any more. If so, the instruction has turned into the resistance it was meant to loosen. Ask instead what feeling is present, what story accompanies it, and what action the circumstances require.

Compare The Presence Process, which gives a structured approach to emotional integration, and The Way of Rest, which emphasises welcoming experience without making its disappearance the goal. Their differences clarify a central question: are you allowing the feeling, or bargaining with it to leave? The latter may reproduce the struggle under a gentler name.
